Doxxing Prevention and What to Do If You’re Doxxed

Updated 27 November 2026 · MyRecon editorial · How we check facts

To reduce the risk of doxxing, review which contact details are public, remove old people-search listings and keep personal accounts separate from public profiles. If your details are already being shared to threaten or harass you, preserve the evidence privately, report the original post and request removal from search where eligible.

What should you do if you’ve been doxxed?

If you believe someone is in immediate physical danger, contact local emergency services first. For ongoing harassment, tell someone you trust and use the reporting resources available where you live. CISA’s guide to responding to doxxing covers documentation and reporting.

  1. Keep a private record. Save the original URL, posting account, date, time and screenshots showing the relevant details or threats. Store them somewhere you control. Share the minimum necessary through the platform’s official reporting channel.
  2. Report the original post or page. Use the platform’s privacy or harassment reporting flow. For a standalone site, use its published abuse or privacy contact if it’s safe to do so. Record the report number if you receive one.
  3. Check the Google Search result separately. Check Google’s personal information and doxxing policy and use its removal form if the result qualifies. Google can remove eligible Search results while the original page remains accessible at its direct URL.
  4. Secure affected accounts. If a password or recovery code was exposed, replace it, review active sessions and enable multifactor authentication. For exposed banking or payment details, contact the provider through a known official channel.

If an unfamiliar caller claims to represent your bank or another service, verify the call using an independent contact.

How can you tell if personal information was removed?

Check the original page and the search result separately. Record a date for each check. Use a signed-out session to check public visibility if doing so is safe. A login prompt or access error leaves the page’s status unresolved.

Fictional example: A platform tells you it removed your phone number from a forum post. Reopen that specific post, verify the number is gone and record the date. If Google’s snippet still shows the number, submit the original post’s URL for a refresh and record that request separately. The report receipt confirms the request was received. Rechecking the page and the snippet tells you what changed.

How can you protect yourself from doxxing?

Review the profiles you already own

Check public bios and old accounts for personal contact details, workplace information and links you no longer want public. Use a separate contact channel for public work where practical. If you use the username checker for a self-audit, confirm each match at the source before treating it as your account. Save what you need before closing accounts you no longer use.

Opt out of people-search listings

Use the listing site’s official opt-out procedure. Start with the sites where you have confirmed your own record. MyRecon has instructions for Spokeo, BeenVerified and Whitepages. The FTC’s people-search guide explains why a listing can reappear and why a broker opt-out leaves underlying public records in place.

Check photos and location sharing

Review what a photo visibly reveals before posting. Turn off camera location access when you don’t need it and check whether the copy you’re about to share includes a location tag. Review who can see your live location, past check-ins and public posts.

Check your domain registration

If you run a personal site, check its public registration fields through ICANN’s registration data lookup. Visible fields vary by registrar and domain. Ask your registrar what its privacy option covers and recheck the public result after any change.

How to protect executives from doxxing

To protect executives from doxxing, audit public leadership bios, company filings, domain WHOIS/RDAP records, breached personal emails, and people-search broker listings before an incident happens. Separate corporate press contact channels from personal phone numbers, home addresses, and family social profiles.

Run quarterly self-audits on each executive's known handles using a digital footprint checker, lock down carrier accounts against SIM swaps with our SIM-swap prevention guide, and keep a pre-approved escalation checklist with Google Search removal links and platform abuse contacts so teams can act within minutes.

For account deletion and longer-term cleanup, follow the personal information removal guide.
