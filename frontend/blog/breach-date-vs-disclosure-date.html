Why a new breach alert can describe an old incident

Updated 25 October 2026 · MyRecon editorial

A breach alert received today can refer to an incident from years ago. Before deciding what happened, separate the date of access, the date the organisation disclosed it, the date a breach catalogue added the record and the date you received an alert. Each answers a different question.

Four dates, four meanings

DateWhat it tells youWhat it cannot prove
Breach dateWhen the recorded incident occurred, sometimes approximatelyThe day your individual record was accessed
Disclosure dateWhen the organisation or source announced the incidentThat access started on that day
Catalogue additionWhen a provider added the incident to its searchable collectionThat the organisation suffered another incident
Your notificationWhen you were informed or a monitor found a changeThat your account was used by somebody else

Read an alert in order

  1. Open the organisation's official notice independently of the message link.
  2. Identify the incident period and whether the date is approximate.
  3. Record which information categories the notice says concern you.
  4. Compare the breach name and incident date with earlier notices before treating it as a second incident.
  5. Write down the action you took and when you will recheck it.

An illustrative example: a company reports a 2022 incident in 2026 and a catalogue adds the record a week later. A daily update should include that newly added record even though the underlying incident is old. Changing its breach date to 2026 would make the timeline less accurate.

How MyRecon labels the record

The public breach API keeps breach_date and added_date separate. The latest-record feed sorts by addition date, while an incident archive can sort by breach date. The feed's generated value describes the publication of that JSON response; it is not the date every incident happened.

These distinctions follow the Have I Been Pwned breach model. Provider coverage is limited to records it has received and published. A daily refresh cannot discover an incident that no source has disclosed.

Turn the timeline into a practical decision

For an old password exposure, establish whether that password was still reused after the incident. For a personal-detail exposure, identify which accounts use those details for recovery. Keep uncertainty visible: an alert records known exposure, while unfamiliar sign-ins require a separate account review. Do not label every alert as a current account takeover.

Continue with reading a breach notification, checking your email, or the breach archive.
