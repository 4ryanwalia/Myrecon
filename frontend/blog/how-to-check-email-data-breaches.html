How to Check if Your Email Was Leaked in a Data Breach (And What to Do)

Updated 13 October 2026 · MyRecon editorial

Billions of user accounts and personal records are exposed in commercial data breaches every year. When cybercriminals breach a database, they often leak email addresses, hashed or plaintext passwords, phone numbers, and physical addresses onto hacker forums and dark web markets.

What happens when a company suffers a data breach?

When an application or service experiences unauthorized database access, threat actors exfiltrate the stored user table. These stolen databases often circulate through several stages:

  1. Private Sale: Initially, the breach data is sold privately among criminal syndicates for targeted extortion or high-value account takeovers.
  2. Combo Lists & Credential Stuffing: Attackers extract email-and-password combinations ("combos") and use automated botnets to test those credentials across hundreds of banking, shopping, and streaming platforms.
  3. Public Disclosure & Leak: Eventually, the entire database is dumped on public forums or distributed via torrents, making it accessible to security researchers and breach intelligence repositories.

How to check if your email was compromised

You can quickly evaluate your email exposure using open breach intelligence databases:

  • Use MyRecon's Email Tool: Enter your email into the MyRecon lookup tool to identify confirmed breaches associated with that address, along with specific exposed data classes (such as passwords, birthdates, or IP addresses).
  • Check Have I Been Pwned: Query Troy Hunt's renowned service at Have I Been Pwned, which indexes over 14 billion compromised records.
  • Inspect Breach Case Files: Read detailed retrospective analyses of major historical breaches in our breach directory and case files library.

The dangers of credential stuffing

The single greatest threat resulting from a data breach is credential stuffing. Over 65% of internet users admit to reusing the same password or variations of it across multiple services. When an attacker acquires a working email and password combination from a breach of an obscure forum, they automatically script login attempts on Gmail, Amazon, PayPal, Netflix, and Apple ID.

If you reused that password anywhere, attackers gain immediate unauthorized access without needing to breach the major provider directly.

How k-anonymity allows safe password checking

You should never type your actual password into a plain website form to check if it has been leaked. Legitimate services use a cryptographic technique called k-anonymity:

  • Your browser computes the SHA-1 hash of your password locally on your device (e.g. 2FD4E1C67A2D28FCED849EE1BB76E7391B93EB12).
  • Only the first 5 characters of the hash (2FD4E) are sent over the network to the database.
  • The database responds with all hash suffixes that begin with those 5 characters (usually several hundred entries).
  • Your device locally checks whether your full hash is in that returned list. The server never sees your password or your complete hash.

5 immediate steps to take if your email was breached

  1. Change the compromised password immediately: Generate a unique, random 16+ character password using a trusted password manager (such as Bitwarden, 1Password, or Apple Keychain).
  2. Update all accounts with reused passwords: Audit your password manager and replace any instance where that password or similar variations were utilized.
  3. Enable Two-Factor Authentication (2FA): Turn on 2FA across your essential accounts. Prioritize authenticator apps (like Aegis, Google Authenticator) or hardware security keys (YubiKey) over SMS verification, which is susceptible to SIM swapping attacks.
  4. Review email forwarding rules: Sign into your email account and verify that no hidden forwarding filters or unauthorized recovery addresses have been created.
  5. Stay vigilant against targeted phishing: Breached personal details are routinely used to craft convincing phishing emails referencing real names and past purchases.
