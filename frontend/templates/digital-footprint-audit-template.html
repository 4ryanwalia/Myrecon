Digital footprint audit template (free spreadsheet)

How a footprint audit works

A digital footprint audit lists every place your personal information appears online, decides what to do about each one, and tracks it until it is fixed. Start from your usernames and email addresses, record each account, breach and public page, mark whether it is really yours, choose keep, edit, close or remove, and set a date to check again.

Download the audit spreadsheet (CSV, 1 KB)   Prefer paper? Download the printable worksheet

Most "footprint audit" results online are marketing audits for brands or a PDF behind a sign-up. This spreadsheet is for one person checking their own exposure. Each row is one account or page, so you can sort by priority and filter for anything still waiting on a provider.

Step 1: list your identifiers

Write down every email address you have used, including old school and work ones, every username or handle, and any phone number you have given to websites. These are the starting points. Most people find two or three forgotten email addresses at this step.

Step 2: find where they appear

  • Run each email through a breach check. MyRecon's free email check and Have I Been Pwned both list named breaches.
  • Run each username through a username search. A free MyRecon account checks 500+ platforms. Open every result yourself: the same handle can belong to someone else.
  • Search your name and city in quotes with search operators. Our search operators guide has ready-made queries.
  • Check people-search sites for your address. The data broker guide explains how listings work and how to opt out.

The download ships with three example rows. They show the three situations you will meet most often: a breached email, an old account you own, and a matching handle that belongs to someone else.

Row 3 is the one people skip, and it is the most important habit in the sheet. A username search will often find accounts that share your handle but are not yours. Recording them as "not mine" stops you from wasting time on them, and from asking a site to delete someone else's account.

A worked example: one evening's audit

Start with two identifiers: your current email and the handle you used in college. The email check lists two named breaches, one from 2019 that included password hashes. That becomes a priority 1 row with the action "change password and enable 2FA", done the same evening, so provider status is "confirmed" and the follow-up date is three months out, to check whether new breaches have appeared.

The handle search reports eleven profiles. You open each one. Four are yours: an old forum account showing your city and birth year (priority 2, "close account"), two dormant social profiles (priority 3, "edit visibility") and a music profile you still use (priority 3, "keep"). Five belong to other people with the same handle and go in as "not mine". Two would not load and are marked "unconfirmed" with a note to retry. After submitting the forum closure, provider status reads "submitted" and search result status "still present", with a follow-up date one month out to check whether the page and its search listing have gone.

At the end you have a sorted list, a record of what you changed and dates to check again, which is what turns a one-off scare into an audit you can repeat.

Step 4: act on the top rows first

Sort by priority. For breaches that included passwords, change that password everywhere you reused it and turn on two-factor authentication. For old accounts, decide between closing and editing: closing removes the source, but search engines can show a cached copy for weeks. Request search removal separately once the source is gone (see removing personal info from search).

Step 5: check again

Footprints grow back. New breaches are disclosed, data brokers re-list addresses and old accounts get reactivated. Put the follow-up dates in your calendar and repeat the whole audit every six to twelve months. Rows marked "not mine" stay in the sheet so you do not investigate them again next time.

Frequently asked questions

What is a digital footprint audit?

A structured review of everywhere your personal information appears online: accounts, breach records, people-search listings and public posts. Each item gets an owner check, an action and a follow-up date.

How often should I audit my digital footprint?

Every six to twelve months, and after any breach notification, job change or move. Data brokers re-list information, so earlier removals need re-checking.

Can I delete my digital footprint completely?

No. You can close accounts, change visibility and request removals, but copies in archives, breach dumps and other people's posts often remain. The aim is to reduce what is easy to find.

Is this audit template free?

Yes. Download the CSV, open it in Excel, Google Sheets or LibreOffice, and keep your completed copy private.
