MyRecon's take

Two details in this record deserve more weight than the total. The first is that analysis indicated the data was scraped rather than taken from a database — collected through interfaces that were working as designed, at a scale nobody intended. The second is that 99% of the addresses had already appeared in earlier breaches.

Both facts cut the same way, and not in the direction most people assume. A scrape means no password was exposed, so there is nothing to reset. A 99% overlap with previous incidents means almost nobody in this file is newly exposed. If you are in it, this is very unlikely to be the breach that matters to you — one of the earlier ones already was.

What it does add is linkage. A username joined to a real name, a country and a confirmed email address is exactly the material used to connect a pseudonymous account to a person. If your chess handle is one you also use elsewhere, that connection is now easier for anyone to make, and no password change undoes it.

What happened

In August 2026, millions of records allegedly sourced from Chess.com were posted online. The data contained 7.3M rows with 4.6M unique email addresses, along with usernames, names, countries and data relating to users' Chess.com accounts. Analysis of the data suggested it had been obtained by scraping. When loaded into HIBP, 99% of the email addresses had already appeared in previous data breaches, further supporting the scraping theory. Read more about scrapes and data breaches.

Who was behind it

No party has been publicly confirmed as responsible, and this page will not name one. Most breaches are never formally attributed: data surfaces on a forum or inside a combined dump long after the intrusion, and the trail back to a specific actor is rarely made public. Where a group has claimed responsibility it is usually named in the account above — that claim is theirs, not a finding of ours.

What was exposed, and why it matters

  • Email addressesThe address becomes a confirmed, active target — expect more phishing, better aimed.
  • Geographic locationsWhere the account was used, which narrows a person down further than most people expect.
  • NamesTurns an anonymous address into an identified person, which is what makes targeted phishing possible.
  • UsernamesA handle links this account to every other place the same handle is used.

What to do if you were in it

  1. Expect better-aimed phishing. A message that already knows your name and where you have an account is the whole point of a breach like this.
