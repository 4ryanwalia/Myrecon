MyRecon's take

No passwords, no payment data, no identity documents, by the standards of this archive the field list is mild. The travel data is what makes it worth reading carefully anyway. Tickets purchased, with routes and fares, is a movement history: where a person goes, how often, in which class, and on which dates.

For most passengers that is a privacy loss rather than a security one. For some it is not. A record of regular journeys between two towns identifies where someone lives and where they work, or who they visit, with a precision that no amount of careful social media hygiene would have given away. Anyone who has moved away from a person they needed to get away from should treat that as the real exposure here.

The immediate practical risk, though, is phone-based. A name, a mobile number and a genuine recent booking is everything a convincing scam call needs, refunds for cancelled trains, fare adjustments, seat upgrades, all referencing a journey the recipient actually took. Indian users in particular have seen sustained campaigns of exactly this shape. No travel operator resolves a refund by asking for a one-time code or a UPI approval; anyone who does is stealing.

The story behind the breach

In December 2022, India’s government-approved online travel agency RailYatri suffered a data breach . The incident impacted over 31M customers and exposed 23M unique email addresses. Also impacted were names, genders, phone numbers and tickets purchased, including travel information and fares.

Breach description from Have I Been Pwned, used under a CC BY 4.0 licence.

What was the outcome?

23,209,732 accounts are listed in this breach. This is a count of accounts, not necessarily individual people.

The reported exposed data includes Email addresses, Genders, Names, Phone numbers, Purchases.

What was exposed, and why it matters

  • Email addressesThe address becomes a confirmed, active target, expect more phishing, better aimed.
  • GendersSmall on its own; useful to anyone assembling a fuller profile.
  • NamesTurns an anonymous address into an identified person, which is what makes targeted phishing possible.
  • Phone numbersEnables SIM-swap attacks against SMS two-factor codes, and puts the number on scam-call lists indefinitely.
  • PurchasesWhat was bought and when, detail that makes an impersonation convincing.

What to do if you were in it

  1. Move two-factor authentication off SMS and onto an authenticator app, which a SIM swap cannot intercept.
  2. Expect better-aimed phishing. A message that already knows your name and where you have an account is the whole point of a breach like this.
