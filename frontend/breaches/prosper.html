On 1 September 2025, Prosper was breached. 17,605,276 accounts were exposed, including browser user agent details, credit status information, dates of birth. MyRecon rates it 74/100, severe. That is about 65% of the population of Australia.

MyRecon's take

Read the accompanying statement carefully: no evidence of unauthorised access to customer accounts. That can be entirely true at the same moment that 17.6 million people's Social Security numbers, incomes, employment status and credit standing have left the building. Account access and data theft are two different claims, and only one of them was being answered.

A lending platform holds the most complete financial identity most people ever assemble in one place, and it holds it for applicants as well as customers. People who were declined, or who filled in the form and thought better of it, have no account, no login, no dashboard and no particular reason to have been watching for a notice. They are in the file all the same.

This is one of the few entries in this archive where the usual advice is insufficient. A government identifier cannot be reissued because it leaked, so the only durable control is limiting what can be opened in your name. A credit freeze at each bureau is free, takes a few minutes, and lifts again when you actually need credit. It is the specific countermeasure for the specific data that left here, and it works whether or not anyone ever touches your Prosper account.

The story behind the breach

In September 2025, Prosper announced that it had detected unauthorised access to their systems, which resulted in the exposure of customer and applicant information . The data breach impacted 17.6M unique email addresses, along with other customer information, including US Social Security numbers. Prosper advised that they did not find any evidence of unauthorised access to customer accounts and funds, and that their customer-facing operations were uninterrupted.

Further information about the incident is contained in Prosper's FAQs .

What was the motive?

The published account does not establish the motive. We cannot tell what the attackers intended from the exposed data alone.

Reported demands or attempts to sell data describe what happened, rather than prove every attacker's intention.

What was the outcome?

17,605,276 accounts are listed in this breach. This is a count of accounts, not necessarily individual people.

The reported exposed data includes Browser user agent details, Credit status information, Dates of birth, Email addresses, Employment statuses, Government issued IDs, Income levels, IP addresses, Names, Physical addresses.

What was exposed, and why it matters

  • Browser user agent detailsAdds detail to a profile that can be assembled from several breaches at once.
  • Credit status informationAdds detail to a profile that can be assembled from several breaches at once.
  • Dates of birthOne of the three things call centres ask to verify identity, and it never changes.
  • Email addressesThe address becomes a confirmed, active target, expect more phishing, better aimed.
  • Employment statusesAdds detail to a profile that can be assembled from several breaches at once.
  • Government issued IDsPassport and licence numbers are used to prove identity to banks and telecoms, and they are effectively permanent.
  • Income levelsAdds detail to a profile that can be assembled from several breaches at once.
  • IP addressesApproximate location at the time of use, and a way to correlate accounts across services.
  • NamesTurns an anonymous address into an identified person, which is what makes targeted phishing possible.
  • Physical addressesWhere someone actually lives. Combined with a name this moves the risk off the internet.

What to do if you were in it

  1. Consider a credit freeze. A government identifier cannot be changed, so limiting what can be opened in your name is the only real control.
  2. Be sceptical of post and callers who already know your address, knowing it is no longer evidence of anything.
  3. Expect better-aimed phishing. A message that already knows your name and where you have an account is the whole point of a breach like this.
