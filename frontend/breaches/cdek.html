MyRecon's take

This is one of the few entries in the archive flagged as unverified, and that flag is the reason it is worth reading. The data was published by a hacktivist collective during a war, with the stated aim of de-anonymising Russian internet users at scale, and its authenticity could not be independently established. It is filed as what it is: an allegation with 19 million addresses attached.

Politically motivated dumps fail differently from criminal ones. A seller has a commercial interest in the data being genuine, because buyers who get fabricated files do not come back. A collective publishing to cause harm to a population has an interest in publication itself, accuracy is a secondary concern, and mixing authentic records with assembled ones costs them nothing and is nearly impossible to disprove from outside.

Both readings carry real risk, which is the uncomfortable part. Courier records are home addresses and delivery histories, about as sensitive as consumer data gets; and for people living under a government that may take an interest in who appears in a hostile dump, being listed is its own hazard whether or not the row is real. Treat an unverified appearance as neither proof that you were breached nor evidence that you were not.

The story behind the breach

In early 2022, a collective known as IT Army whose stated goal is to "completely de-anonymise most Russian users by leaking hundreds of gigabytes of databases" published over 30GB of data allegedly sourced from Russian courier service CDEK. The data contained over 19M unique email addresses along with names and phone numbers. The authenticity of the breach could not be independently established and has been flagged as "unverified".

How did it happen?

The published account does not explain the exact way the data was obtained.

What was the motive?

The published account does not establish the motive. We cannot tell what the attackers intended from the exposed data alone.

Reported demands or attempts to sell data describe what happened, rather than prove every attacker's intention.

What was the outcome?

19,218,203 accounts are listed in this breach. This is a count of accounts, not necessarily individual people.

The reported exposed data includes Email addresses, Names, Phone numbers.

What was exposed, and why it matters

  • Email addressesThe address becomes a confirmed, active target, expect more phishing, better aimed.
  • NamesTurns an anonymous address into an identified person, which is what makes targeted phishing possible.
  • Phone numbersEnables SIM-swap attacks against SMS two-factor codes, and puts the number on scam-call lists indefinitely.

What to do if you were in it

  1. Move two-factor authentication off SMS and onto an authenticator app, which a SIM swap cannot intercept.
  2. Expect better-aimed phishing. A message that already knows your name and where you have an account is the whole point of a breach like this.

Use the email box on this page. The result applies only to this incident. If the provider does not cover this breach, we say so. A missing match does not prove you were unaffected.

What data was leaked in the CDEK breach?

email addresses, names, phone numbers. Each one is explained above, along with what it means for the person it belongs to.

When did it happen, and when did it become public?

The breach is dated 9 March 2022. It was published to Have I Been Pwned on 17 March 2022, a gap of 8 days during which the data was already out.

Is the CDEK breach real?

It is listed as unverified. The data exists and has been indexed, but it has not been confirmed against the named source, so treat the origin as unproven.

How many people were affected?

19,218,203 accounts. That is about 71% of the population of Australia. That is accounts rather than people, one person often has several.
