MyRecon's take

Genesis Market is the entry in this archive that best explains why changing your password is sometimes not enough. The service did not primarily trade passwords: it traded browser fingerprints and session cookies, the tokens that tell a website you have already logged in, bundled with enough device detail to make the impersonation look like your own machine.

A buyer could load that package and appear to a service as the victim, on the victim's apparent device, already authenticated. No password prompt, and in many cases no two-factor challenge either, because the session had already passed it. This is why security advice increasingly includes "sign out of all sessions everywhere" after a compromise, which is a control most people have never used.

The other thing to take from this record is where the data came from. Genesis Market's inventory was harvested from infected consumer devices, which means the victims were compromised personally rather than through any company they dealt with. The site was taken down in a coordinated international operation in 2023, and the underlying trade did not stop, the marketplaces changed names.

If you are in this file, treat the device as the problem rather than the account. Clean or rebuild it, then revoke active sessions on your important services, then change passwords, in that order, because doing it the other way round means handing your new password to whatever is still watching.

What happened

In April 2023, the stolen identity marketplace Genesis Market was shut down by the FBI and a coalition of law enforcement agencies across the globe in "Operation Cookie Monster". The service traded in "browser fingerprints" which enabled criminals to impersonate victims and access their online services. As many of the impacted accounts did not include email addresses, "8M" is merely an approximation intended to indicate scale. Other personal data compromised by the service included names, addresses and credit card information, although not all individuals had each of these fields exposed.

Who was behind it

No party has been publicly confirmed as responsible, and this page will not name one. Most breaches are never formally attributed: data surfaces on a forum or inside a combined dump long after the intrusion, and the trail back to a specific actor is rarely made public. Where a group has claimed responsibility it is usually named in the account above, that claim is theirs, not a finding of ours.

What was exposed, and why it matters

  • Browser user agent detailsAdds detail to a profile that can be assembled from several breaches at once.
  • Credit card CVVAdds detail to a profile that can be assembled from several breaches at once.
  • Credit cardsDirectly spendable until the card is cancelled, and card numbers circulate for years after a breach.
  • Dates of birthOne of the three things call centres ask to verify identity, and it never changes.
  • Email addressesThe address becomes a confirmed, active target, expect more phishing, better aimed.
  • NamesTurns an anonymous address into an identified person, which is what makes targeted phishing possible.
  • PasswordsAnyone reusing this password elsewhere has those accounts exposed too, credential stuffing is automated and tries them all within days.
  • Phone numbersEnables SIM-swap attacks against SMS two-factor codes, and puts the number on scam-call lists indefinitely.
  • Physical addressesWhere someone actually lives. Combined with a name this moves the risk off the internet.
  • UsernamesA handle links this account to every other place the same handle is used.

What to do if you were in it

  1. Change this password anywhere you reused it, starting with your email account, that is the one that can reset all the others.
  2. Move two-factor authentication off SMS and onto an authenticator app, which a SIM swap cannot intercept.
  3. Check statements for the months after the breach date and ask your bank for a replacement card if the number was full.
  4. Be sceptical of post and callers who already know your address, knowing it is no longer evidence of anything.
  5. Expect better-aimed phishing. A message that already knows your name and where you have an account is the whole point of a breach like this.
