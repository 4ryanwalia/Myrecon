MyRecon's take

This belongs to a set rather than standing alone. Through mid-2024 a long run of large companies disclosed breaches traced to their own cloud data warehouse tenants, accounts reached with credentials stolen earlier by malware, on tenants where multi-factor authentication was not enforced. The platform itself was not compromised. The customers' configurations of it were.

It is the clearest illustration available of what shared responsibility means in practice. The provider secures the service; the customer secures access to their instance. Where that line sits is written in documentation nobody reads until a quarter of a billion records have moved, and in 2024 a great many organisations discovered which side of it they had been standing on.

The full case file covers how the campaign worked and why so many companies fell in the same few weeks.

The record says the file covered both customers and employees, and those two groups face different problems. For customers, the fields are the familiar ones: name, email, phone number and postal address, enough for a convincing message about an order, a rewards balance or a recall notice on a car part. For employees, HIBP notes further attributes related to company staff without listing them, so anyone who worked there should be wary of messages about pay, tax forms or benefits rather than only shopping scams.

Auto parts give the phishing a specific shape. A message about a warranty claim, a part that has been recalled, or a battery or tyre order is believable precisely because so many people buy from a parts chain once or twice a year and do not remember the details. If you are in this file, treat recall and warranty notices with extra care, and check them against the manufacturer or the official recall service for your country rather than replying to whoever sent the message.

The timing is also typical of the 2024 cloud-warehouse cases. The breach date is 5 June and HIBP added the data on 24 June, after it had already been posted for sale. When the first public sign of a breach is a sales listing, the people in the data have been exposed for at least as long as the listing has existed, which is why the advice in these cases is always to assume the details are already in use.

The story behind the breach

In June 2024, Advance Auto Parts confirmed they had suffered a data breach which was posted for sale to a popular hacking forum. Linked to unauthorised access to Snowflake cloud services, the breach exposed a large number of records related to both customers and employees. In total, 79M unique email addresses were included in the breach, alongside names, phone numbers, addresses and further data attributes related to company employees.

What was exposed, and why it matters

  • Email addressesAn exposed address may be used in phishing attempts. Treat unexpected messages with caution.
  • NamesTurns an anonymous address into an identified person, which is what makes targeted phishing possible.
  • Phone numbersEnables SIM-swap attacks against SMS two-factor codes, and puts the number on scam-call lists indefinitely.
  • Physical addressesWhere someone actually lives. Combined with a name this moves the risk off the internet.

What to do if you were in it

  1. Move two-factor authentication off SMS and onto an authenticator app, which a SIM swap cannot intercept.
  2. Be sceptical of post and callers who already know your address, knowing it is no longer evidence of anything.
  3. Be alert to phishing. A message may use details from exposed or collected records to appear familiar; verify requests through the service directly.
