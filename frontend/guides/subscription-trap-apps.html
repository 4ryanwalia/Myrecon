Subscription Trap Apps: How to Cancel and Get a Refund

Published 21 September 2026 · 7 min read

A free trial you could not use, a weekly charge you did not expect, and an app that will not show you how to stop it. This is a design pattern, not an accident, and there is a defined way out of it. Cancel first, then ask for the money back, in that order, because they are two separate processes and only one of them is urgent.

Do this first

Deleting the app does not cancel the subscription. The subscription is held by Google Play or the App Store, not by the app, and billing continues after you remove it from your phone. Cancel in the store's own subscriptions screen. This single misunderstanding is why people discover they have been paying for two years for something they deleted in week one.

Cancelling stops future charges. Getting an existing charge back is a separate request, covered below.

Cancel the billing

On Android. Open the Play Store, tap your profile picture in the top right, then Payments and subscriptions → Subscriptions. Select the app and tap Cancel subscription. You can also do it from a browser at play.google.com/store/account/subscriptions, which is often faster and harder for an app to interfere with.

On iPhone or iPad. Open Settings, tap your name at the top, then Subscriptions. Select the app and tap Cancel Subscription. Alternatively: App Store → your profile picture → Subscriptions.

Then go back and check. The entry should now show an expires on date instead of a renews on date. If it still says renews, the cancellation did not complete. You normally keep access until the end of the period you have already paid for, so there is no reason to delay cancelling.

If the subscription was bought inside the app with a card rather than through the store, it will not appear in either list. Check your card statement for the merchant name and cancel through the developer's own website.

Ask for the money back

Refunds are discretionary. Asking promptly and plainly works more often than people expect.

Google Play. Go to play.google.com/store/account/orderhistory, find the charge, and use Report a problem. Requests made within 48 hours of the charge are handled most readily by Google directly; after that the request is generally routed to the developer, who sets their own policy. Choose the reason that is actually true and write one or two specific sentences, that you were charged after a trial you could not use, or that the app did not function.

Apple. Go to reportaproblem.apple.com and sign in with your Apple Account. Find the charge and request a refund. Apple publishes no fixed window and reviews case by case, so it is worth asking even for an older charge.

If the store declines, email the developer. Every store listing carries a contact address. State the charge date, the amount, and that you have cancelled. Keep it factual and keep a copy, a written record is what makes the next step possible.

As a last resort, your bank. If the charge was genuinely unauthorised, or you were actively prevented from cancelling, contact your card issuer about a chargeback and show them your cancellation attempt and correspondence. Use this properly: disputing a charge you did authorise and did get value from can get your store account restricted, which affects every other app and purchase on it.

The patterns worth recognising

Naming these makes them much easier to spot in the two seconds you actually have.

Weekly billing. A weekly price reads as trivial and totals to far more than a comparable monthly product across a year. It also shortens the interval in which cancelling feels urgent, so a lapse costs you another cycle almost immediately.

The unusable trial. A free trial is offered, but every meaningful action inside it is gated behind the paid tier. The trial exists to capture the payment method, not to demonstrate the product.

The blurred result. A count or a greyed-out list shown before payment. Producing "14 results found" requires no lookup to have happened at all, and frequently none has. Treat it as an advertisement until you have seen one complete item you can verify yourself.

The review prompt before first use. Some apps ask for a rating during onboarding, before you have used anything. A rating collected at that moment measures the onboarding screen, and it is part of why store ratings on these apps can look healthy while recent reviews do not.

The buried exit. No visible subscription management inside the app, or a settings screen that links out to a help article instead of the cancellation. The store's own subscriptions screen bypasses this entirely, which is why it is the first step above.

Reporting it

Both stores require subscription price and billing period to be disclosed clearly before purchase, and consumer law in the EU, UK, India and many other jurisdictions sets comparable requirements. Whether any particular app has broken those rules is for the store or a regulator to judge, but reports are how they find out.

On Google Play, scroll to the bottom of the listing and use Flag as inappropriate. On the App Store, use Report a Problem on the product page. Leaving a specific, factual review helps the next person more than a one-star with no detail: say what you paid, what the billing period was, and what the app did or did not return.

National consumer bodies also accept complaints about unclear subscription terms, and in several countries that route has produced refunds where a store request did not.

Avoiding the next one

  • Set the reminder when you start the trial, for the day before it ends. The model depends on you not doing this.
  • Read the billing period, not the price. "₹699" means very different things weekly and yearly.
  • Check top grossing versus top free. A high top-grossing rank tells you the subscription converts well. It tells you nothing about whether the app works.
  • Read the newest reviews, not the most helpful ones. The default sort shows settled older reviews; newest shows the current build and current billing behaviour.
  • Prefer tools that show you a real result first. If a product cannot demonstrate one complete, checkable finding before asking for a card, that is information about the product.

Common questions

Does deleting the app cancel the subscription?

No. The subscription lives with Google Play or the App Store, not the app, and billing continues after you delete it. Cancel in the store's subscriptions screen. This is the most common reason people keep paying for an app they removed long ago.

Can I get a refund after a free trial converts?

Often, especially if you ask quickly. Google Play handles requests within 48 hours of the charge most readily; beyond that it routes to the developer. Apple publishes no fixed window and reviews case by case. Refunds are discretionary in both cases, so a clear, specific request helps.

Why are these priced per week?

Because a weekly figure reads as small while totalling to a large annual amount, and a short billing period leaves less time to notice and cancel.

Is an app that does this breaking the rules?

Both stores require clear disclosure of subscription terms before purchase, and consumer law in many countries requires the same. Whether a specific app breaches them is for the store or a regulator to decide, but you can report it, and both stores have a reporting option on the listing.

How do I avoid this next time?

Set a calendar reminder the moment a trial starts. Read the billing period rather than the headline price. Check top grossing versus top free. And prefer tools that show a complete, verifiable result before asking for payment.
