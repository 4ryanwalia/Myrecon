Email intelligence

Check your email for breach exposure and read the result

Updated 1 November 2026 · MyRecon editorial

Use an address you control to check supported breach sources. Review the named incidents, exposed data categories and source status before choosing an account-security action. An email match does not prove your mailbox is currently compromised, and no returned match does not prove the address has never leaked.

Open the email breach checker

Run an email breach check step by step

  1. Select Email in the tool. Enter an address you own or are authorized to check. Do not put a password in the email field.
  2. Choose the scope. Leave “Check linked accounts” off for this breach-focused walkthrough. Turning it on sends the address to additional responding services for account signals; it is a separate check.
  3. Press Investigate. Read source status as well as returned findings. A rate limit, disabled source or failed request leaves a coverage gap.
  4. Review any named incident. Record the provider, breach name, incident date, reported data categories and review date. Compilations and historical associations require context.
  5. Open the affected service directly. Confirm your account, inspect security settings and follow its official incident advice. Keep a lookup result separate from proof of a live account takeover.

MyRecon is useful when you want supported email findings and source-status context alongside a broader footprint review. For provider-specific breach details or verified-owner views, consult the source service itself. Public username discovery is a separate workflow in the browser username guide.

Current access and source limits

Reviewed 1 November 2026: email lookup results are visible without sign-in or a paid plan and do not spend your username scan allowance. Source rate limits and outages still apply. Optional integrations depend on configuration and provider access; buying a username scan pack does not guarantee that every email source will respond. See current product access.

MyRecon's email workflow uses supported sources including LeakCheck and XposedOrNot. Have I Been Pwned is a separate service to consult directly, not a promise that MyRecon uses its paid email API. Its FAQ explains that sensitive breaches require verified-owner access. XposedOrNot's FAQ describes its own coverage and privacy controls. Different sources can return different sets of records.

An email lookup sends the address to the providers needed for the chosen checks. It is different from a password-range lookup that sends a hash prefix. Read MyRecon's privacy policy before submitting an identifier. A public breach catalogue lists incidents; browsing that catalogue does not check whether your individual address appears.

Read the result before choosing an action

  • Named breach: the source reports an association with that dataset. Review its provenance and data categories. Historical exposure is not proof that an account is still open or that your current password leaked.
  • No match after completed checks: no match was returned by the checked sources at that time. Unpublished, unindexed, restricted or privacy-suppressed records can be missing.
  • Partial, unavailable or rate-limited: retain completed findings, but record the failed checks separately. Retry later or review a provider directly; do not label the address safe.
  • Linked-service or registration signal: this is a different evidence type. A historical service name, a public profile and a registration response do not all establish the same thing or authenticate ownership.

Dates describe different events: the incident, public disclosure and addition to a provider's catalogue can happen at different times. Prioritize the exposed fields and whether you still reuse an affected credential. An old password still in use can matter more than a newer email-only record.

What to do with a supported breach finding

  1. Review the provider's incident advice. Navigate to the official service independently. Be cautious of messages demanding payment or a login through an unfamiliar link.
  2. Replace affected reused passwords. Use unique passwords on the affected service and elsewhere you reused that credential. A reported password data category does not reveal your actual current password.
  3. Review stronger sign-in options. Enable available multifactor authentication or passkeys. Inspect active sessions, recovery details and forwarding rules where relevant.
  4. Match the response to the exposed information. Contact details call for phishing awareness; other sensitive fields may require the affected provider's specific response process.
  5. Record the action and follow up. Note what changed and when. A breach record may remain after you secure or close an account.

A separate password-exposure check answers whether that password has appeared in supported leak data; it does not identify which email or service used it. Do not share your password in this guide, a report or an email lookup.

Email breach questions

Is MyRecon's email breach check free?

Returned email findings are visible without sign-in or payment and do not spend the username scan allowance. Provider availability and rate limits still apply.

Does zero named breaches mean my email is safe?

No. Check coverage and source status first. Even completed checks only cover the records those sources can return at that time.

Does a breach match mean someone can access my mailbox now?

No. It reports source-linked historical exposure, not proof of current mailbox access. Review affected credentials and the provider's security information.
