Data Breach Settlement Claims: How to Tell Real From Fake

Published 25 November 2026 · 10 min read

After a large breach, two kinds of message tend to follow. One is a genuine notice that a settlement exists and you may be able to claim from it. The other is a scam dressed up as that notice, built to collect your bank details or Social Security number. They can look almost the same. This guide explains how breach settlements actually work in the US, what a real notice must contain, what the FTC says it will never ask for, and a short routine for checking any claim before you fill it in.

The short version

  • You should never have to pay to file a claim. The FTC said so plainly about the Equifax settlement, and it says it "never requires you to pay upfront fees" in its own refund programs.
  • Do not start from the link in the message. Find the settlement independently, through the court case or, for FTC refunds, ftc.gov/refunds, and type the address yourself.
  • Expect small payouts. In the Equifax settlement, the largest in US consumer data history, the advertised $125 cash option was cut to a few dollars because far more people claimed than the fund could pay.

This is general information about US settlements, not legal advice. Sources: Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23, the FTC's refund programs FAQ and consumer alerts, and MyRecon's Equifax case file, read 25 November 2026.

Two different things called a "settlement"

Most confusion starts here. A breach can lead to money for affected people through two separate routes, and they work differently.

Class action settlements compared with FTC refund programs.
Class action settlementFTC refund program
Who brings the casePrivate lawyers on behalf of a group (the "class")The Federal Trade Commission, a government agency
Who approves itA court, after a hearingAn order in the FTC's case, typically from a court
Do you need to file a claim?Usually yes, before a deadlineThe FTC says most of its cases "do not require you to file a claim"; it pays from the company's customer list

Some breaches produce both. Equifax's 2017 breach ended in a settlement with the FTC, the CFPB and 50 US states and territories worth up to $700 million, including a $425 million fund for consumers. See the Equifax case file for how that played out.

What a real class action notice must say

In US federal court, Rule 23 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure sets out what class members must be told. For the kind of class used in most money claims, the court must direct "the best notice that is practicable under the circumstances", which can go "by one or more of the following: United States mail, electronic means, or other appropriate means". The notice must state, in plain language:

  1. the nature of the action;
  2. the definition of the class certified;
  3. the class claims, issues, or defenses;
  4. that a class member may enter an appearance through an attorney if they want to;
  5. that the court will exclude any member who requests exclusion;
  6. the time and manner for requesting exclusion; and
  7. the binding effect of a class judgment on members.

A message that only says "you are owed money, click here" and leaves out who the parties are, which court, and your right to opt out does not look like a court-directed notice. That is not proof of a scam on its own, because a reminder email can be shorter than the formal notice, but it is a reason to go and find the formal notice yourself.

Rule 23 also says a settlement that binds the class can be approved "only after a hearing and only on finding that it is fair, reasonable, and adequate", and that any class member may object. So a real settlement has a court, a case, and a public record you can check.

What the FTC says it will never do

For its own refund programs, the FTC lists the signs of a real payment or claim form and the things it never asks for:

  • A real FTC payment or claim form "will include an explanation and details about the case", and the case will be listed at ftc.gov/refunds "along with the name of the company issuing payments and a phone number for questions".
  • The FTC "never requires you to pay upfront fees or asks you for sensitive information, like your Social Security number or bank account information".
  • It currently pays by check, prepaid debit card, PayPal and Zelle. When it uses PayPal, it first sends an email from an address ending in .gov, and advises against clicking links in emails that seem to come from the FTC or PayPal.
  • Its refunds page warns that it will never threaten you, tell you to transfer money to "get a refund", or promise a prize.

The FTC also named the five companies it currently contracts with for refunds: Analytics Consulting, Epiq Systems, JND Legal Administration, Rust Consulting and Simpluris. That list applies to FTC refund programs only. Private class action settlements can use other administrators, so a company missing from this list is not by itself a sign of fraud.

When the Equifax settlement opened, the FTC warned that fake websites imitating the official claims site had already started to appear, told people to start at its own page, and added that "anyone who calls and tries to get you to file a claim is almost certainly a scammer".

How to check a settlement claim in five steps

  1. Get the case name, not just the company name. A real notice names the lawsuit (for example "In re [Company] Data Breach Litigation") and the court. If the message does not, search for the company name with "data breach settlement" and look for news coverage or court records that name the case.
  2. Find the settlement website independently. Take the address from the court-approved notice, a court record, the company's own breach notice page, or, for FTC refunds, ftc.gov/refunds. Type it in yourself. Compare it carefully with the link in the message you received.
  3. Check what they ask for. Claim forms usually ask for your name, contact details, a claim or notice ID if you were sent one, and how you want to be paid. Treat any request for an upfront fee, a full card number with security code, or an account password as a stop sign. If a form asks for something sensitive, confirm on the independently found site that the real form asks for it too.
  4. Check the deadline and the dates. Real settlements have a claim deadline, an opt-out deadline and a final approval hearing date. Pressure to act "today" or "within 24 hours" is a scam pattern.
  5. Phone the number from the official listing, not the message. For FTC programs the FTC says to call the number on the claim form or check, and that you can verify those numbers at ftc.gov/refunds.

What you can usually claim, and what to expect

Each settlement defines its own benefits in the notice. Read that list rather than an advert's summary of it. Two lessons from Equifax are worth knowing before you spend time on any claim:

  • Headline cash amounts are often a ceiling. The FTC had to warn claimants that the $125 cash alternative would be reduced, because far more people chose it than the fund could pay. Most people received a few dollars.
  • Some benefits outlast the claim deadline. The Equifax claim deadline was 22 January 2024, but the FTC says affected people who discover identity misuse can use free identity restoration help until January 2029, even if they never filed a claim.

Filing a claim also does not fix the exposure. If your details were in the breach, the protective steps matter more than the payout: freeze your credit, change reused passwords, and watch for phishing that uses the breach as bait. Our guide to reading a breach notification covers what to do first.

Common settlement scam patterns

  • The "processing fee". You are told a payout is waiting but a small fee is due first. Real settlements and FTC refunds do not charge you to claim.
  • The look-alike claims site. A domain one word different from the real settlement site, often promoted by ads or text messages, that collects Social Security and bank numbers.
  • The helpful caller. Someone phones offering to file your claim for you. The FTC says such callers are almost certainly scammers.
  • The fake government notice. An email or letter claiming to be from the FTC that threatens you, promises a prize, or asks for a money transfer. The FTC says it does none of those things.
  • The breach-themed phish. A message that uses a real, recent breach to make a login page or "verify your identity" request look plausible. Check the breach itself first in the MyRecon breach archive, then go to the company directly.

If you have already entered bank or card details on a site you now doubt, contact your bank straight away. In the US, you can report it to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov.

Outside the US

This guide covers US settlements, where most breach class actions are filed. Other countries handle compensation differently, and many have no equivalent process. If you are outside the US and receive a "settlement" message about a company you have never dealt with in the US, treat it with extra suspicion. Common questions

Do I have to pay to file a data breach settlement claim?

No. The FTC told Equifax claimants they would never have to pay to file a claim, and it says its own refund programs never require upfront fees. A request for a fee to release a payout is a scam sign.

How do I know if a data breach settlement email is real?

Do not use the link in the email. Find the case name and court, then reach the settlement website through a court record, the company's own notice or, for FTC refunds, ftc.gov/refunds, and compare it with the email.

Will a settlement ask for my Social Security number?

The FTC says its refund programs never ask for your Social Security number or bank account information. Some class action claim forms ask for identifying details to confirm eligibility, so check on the independently found official site that the real form asks for the same thing.

How much money will I get from a data breach settlement?

Often very little. Payouts depend on the fund size and how many people claim. In the Equifax settlement, the advertised $125 cash option was cut to a few dollars because the fund was heavily oversubscribed.
