"Survey results" is the quietest data class in this archive and one of the most revealing. People answer surveys candidly precisely because they believe the answers are aggregated and anonymous. Tie those answers back to an email address and an IP address, as this data does, and the assumption that made them candid is the thing that makes the file valuable.

The employee engagement survey, the health questionnaire, the political polling, the customer satisfaction form that asked why you left, all of it is opinion given under an expectation of distance from the person holding it.

Worth noting too how this record's status changed. It sat flagged as unverified because the company would not confirm a breach, and the flag was removed only after people found their own accurate records in it. A company declining to confirm does not make a dataset false, and the verification eventually came from the people in it rather than from the organisation holding it.

HIBP also notes that some of the email addresses appear to have been generated by the platform itself. That detail matters for anyone checking their own address: a survey service may create placeholder or anonymised respondent identifiers, so the presence of an odd-looking address in the count does not inflate the real exposure much, while the presence of your own address means a survey you answered was linked to you more directly than the form suggested.

Two of the fields, IP addresses and browser user agents, are the technical fingerprint of the moment someone submitted a response. For most respondents that is low risk on its own. For anyone who answered a workplace survey from an office network, it can be enough to tie an "anonymous" response back to a team or a building. If you have ever answered a sensitive survey run through a third-party tool, this is a reminder to read the anonymity promise as a statement about the report the organiser sees, not about what the platform itself stores.
