This is one of a run of entries in the archive with the same shape: a ShinyHunters "pay or leak" demand, followed by publication of what is mostly corporate contact data. Neogen's set is mailing lists, employee records and business contacts, so most people in it are not customers in the consumer sense. They are buyers, suppliers, lab staff and people who once filled in a form.

Contact records sound harmless until you look at what they combine: name, salutation, employer, job title, direct phone number and work address. That is the exact kit needed for a convincing business email compromise. An invoice that appears to come from a real supplier, addressed to the right person by title, and followed up by a phone call to their actual desk number is far more persuasive than a generic phishing email.

If your work address is in this set, the people to warn are colleagues in accounts payable. Any request to change a supplier's bank details, especially one that arrives with urgency, should be confirmed by calling a number you already had on file, not one in the message.

Neogen works in food safety and animal health, so the business contacts in this file sit in supply chains that run on trust and paperwork: laboratories, farms, food manufacturers and distributors. A fake certificate request, a fake test-kit order or a fake change to a recall notice would reach people who handle exactly those documents every week, and the leaked job titles make it easy to send each message to the right desk.

Salutations appear in the field list too, which is a small but telling detail. A message that addresses someone as "Dr." or by their correct title reads as if it came from someone who knows them, and formality is one of the cues people use to judge whether an email is genuine. Teams in this file can blunt that by agreeing that urgent document or payment requests, however correctly addressed, are confirmed by phone before anyone acts on them.
