There is an irony in a company-intelligence platform losing its own contact data. Crunchbase is in the business of organising information about businesses and the people in them, and what leaked was largely its leads and business contacts: names, employers, job titles, phone numbers, addresses and LinkedIn profiles.

The LinkedIn field is what makes this set unusually ready to use. Most leaked contact lists need some work before an attacker can be sure the person and the job are current. A profile link does that work: it confirms the role, shows the reporting line and supplies the photo and history needed to impersonate a colleague or a recruiter convincingly.

Crunchbase later confirmed that documents were exfiltrated from its corporate network, so this is not only a scraped list. Founders, investors and executives are over-represented in a dataset like this, and they are the usual targets for fake investment offers, fake board introductions and invoice fraud. If you are in it, be sceptical of unsolicited deal flow that seems to know exactly who you are and who you work with.

The date gap is also unusually long for a 2026 incident. The extortion attempt took place in January, and the data reached HIBP in October. For nine months a curated list of business contacts with their roles and LinkedIn profiles was available to whoever had it, and the people in it are precisely the ones who receive cold outreach every day, which makes a targeted approach hard to tell from normal business traffic.

For companies whose staff appear in the file, the most effective response is procedural rather than individual. Agree internally that changes to payment details, wire instructions or investment terms are confirmed by a call to a known number, and make it normal for staff to ask for that confirmation even when a request appears to come from a founder or an investor they know. The leaked fields make impersonation easy. A rule like that makes it slow, and slow is usually enough to stop it.
