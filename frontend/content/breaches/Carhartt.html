The published corpus contained millions of synthetic records, fabricated rows matching no real person, which were identified and excluded before the figure of 12.9 million was settled on. That detail is worth more than the breach itself, because the number an extortion crew posts and the number of people actually in the file are routinely different numbers, and the first one is the one that becomes the headline.

Padding pays twice. A larger file sells for more, and a company forced to answer for an inflated figure looks worse than it is, which is useful leverage when the demand is "pay or we publish". Establishing what is actually in a dump is slow, unglamorous work, and a breach story that leads with the raw row count is a story where nobody has done it yet.

What genuinely leaked here is ordinary: name, email address, phone number, home address. No passwords, no card numbers, nothing that opens an account. That combination does not get into anything, it gets a convincing message about a delivery problem on an order you could plausibly have placed. The uptick in parcel texts after a retail breach is not a coincidence, and it is the whole return on data like this.
