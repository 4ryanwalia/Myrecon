Twenty-three thousand websites. That number is the whole story, because almost none of them are sites you would remember using. The forum you joined once in 2013, the shop that sold one thing you needed, the local club, the game that shut down, small sites with small security budgets, breached individually and unremarkably, then swept into one collection.

Nobody notified anyone. Most of those sites never knew, and a good number no longer existed by the time the collection was published. There is no company here to be accountable, no disclosure statement, and no reset email coming.

The compilation also carried cracked plain-text passwords alongside the hashes, which removes the last bit of friction. If you find yourself in this one, the question is not which site leaked it, that is usually unanswerable, but where else that password was ever used. That is the only question this data makes it possible to act on.

The record flags this collection as unverified, and that status is honest rather than reassuring. With twenty-three thousand sources, nobody could check each one against the site it supposedly came from. Independent analysis found many genuine, previously undisclosed breaches inside it, which means the right reading for an individual is simple: if your address and a password you recognise appear together, the pairing is real whether or not the site it came from can ever be named.

A compilation of small sites also changes who is exposed. Large companies tend to force password resets after a breach. A defunct forum or a one-person shop does not, so the passwords in this file were never invalidated at the source, and many of the accounts still exist in some form. The useful exercise is a short inventory: which old services might still have an account under that email, which of them hold anything that matters, and which can simply be deleted. Our guide to auditing a digital footprint walks through finding forgotten accounts, and every deleted account is one less place an old password can be tried.
