A florist's database has a property most retail data does not: a large share of the people in it never bought anything. Gift orders carry a recipient, a name, an address, often a phone number, belonging to somebody who has no account, agreed to no terms, and will never receive a notification because the company has no relationship with them to notify.

Whether this particular corpus separates buyers from recipients is not something the breach record can tell you, and it is worth being precise about that rather than assuming the worst. What the record does say is that 3.2 million addresses appeared alongside names, phone numbers, physical addresses and partial card data, in a file offered for sale roughly five months after the most recent data in it was created. Nobody was watching in between.

The exposure that follows is a delivery scam, and flowers make it unusually effective. An unexpected delivery is plausible for almost anyone, confirming an address for one feels harmless, and a message that already has your address and the last four digits of a card reads as legitimate. A courier that cannot deliver does not need your card details to try again, and a florist does not ask for them by text.
