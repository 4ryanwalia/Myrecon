Read the disclosure language and the outcome side by side. The company described "a limited set of data from a webpage hosted by Salesforce on its platform". More than 100GB was subsequently published, carrying 13.5 million distinct email addresses. Both statements can be technically accurate at the same time, one describes the surface that was exposed, the other describes what came through it, and that is exactly why a description of the entry point is not a measure of the damage.

The other thing worth noticing is whose data this is. Students and instructors did not choose McGraw Hill; an institution chose it for them, and the record that ended up in a 100GB archive was created as a condition of taking a course. That pattern runs through most education technology breaches, and it is why "stop using the service" is advice that does not apply here.

Physical addresses and phone numbers appear only in some of the records here, which HIBP describes as appearing inconsistently across files. That matters for anyone checking whether they are affected. Finding your email in the data does not tell you how much else was attached to it, and not finding your postal address in one copy does not mean it was absent from another. When the source is a set of distributed files rather than one database export, the honest assumption is the fuller one.

The audience shapes the scams. Education publishers hold accounts for students, instructors and school administrators, and the messages that follow look like course access problems, expiring textbook licences, refunds for digital materials, or an instructor asking students to log in somewhere new before an assignment is due. Students are a particularly exposed group, because they are used to being sent to unfamiliar login pages by their courses and many are handling their first accounts of this kind.

If you teach, this is worth a sentence to your class: course platforms do not ask for passwords or payment details by email, and any change to where students log in will come through the institution's own systems. If you are a student in the file, the most useful step is to check whether the email address on your McGraw Hill account is your school address or a personal one, because that decides which inbox the follow-up phishing will land in.
