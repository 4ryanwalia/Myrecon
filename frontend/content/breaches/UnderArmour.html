The size is not the interesting part. What matters here is the two-month gap: the ransomware group claimed the intrusion in November, and the data only reached a public forum in January. For anyone in that file, the exposure began long before there was anything to read about it.

That gap is the norm rather than the exception, which is why "we have seen no evidence of misuse" in a disclosure statement means considerably less than it sounds like.

Look at which fields sit together in this file: date of birth, gender, a geographic location and purchase information, attached to a name and an email address. That is not a login record. It is a customer profile of the kind a retailer builds for marketing, and it describes a person in more detail than most account dumps do. Someone holding it knows roughly where you live, how old you are, and what you bought from a sportswear brand, which is enough to write a message that sounds exactly like the company.

The fraud that follows a retail file like this one is rarely clever. It is an order confirmation you did not place, a delivery that needs a small redelivery fee, a refund for a product you really did buy, or a loyalty reward that expires today. Each of those works better when the sender can quote a real purchase. If you shopped with Under Armour, the defence is to stop treating a correct order detail as proof that a message is genuine, and to check orders by typing the retailer's address yourself rather than following a link.

The record also lists no passwords, and that is worth saying plainly because it changes what helps. Resetting your Under Armour password is harmless but does not touch the problem. The exposure here is personal detail that cannot be changed, so the response is attention over time: a date of birth paired with your name and location is a standard verification question, and it is worth asking your bank or phone carrier whether they still accept it on its own.
