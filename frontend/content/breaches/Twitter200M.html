This one is dangerous for a reason the account count does not convey. The API that was abused resolved an email address to a profile. The resulting file therefore does something no ordinary credential dump does: it links a pseudonymous handle to a real email address.

For most people that is an annoyance. For anyone whose account was deliberately separated from their identity, someone posting under a pseudonym about a government, an employer, an abusive partner, a religion they left, or a diagnosis, it is the specific harm they built the separation to prevent. The link is not reversible, the file is public, and a password change does nothing about it.

If that applies to you, the useful response is not account hygiene. It is to check which email address the pseudonymous account was registered with, assume the connection is known, and decide whether the account should continue under that identity at all.

The dates in the record show how long a quiet exposure can last. The API abuse took place in 2021, and the corpus surfaced on a hacking forum in early 2023, roughly two years later. Throughout that time the linkage between handles and email addresses existed in private hands, and nothing visible to the account holders had changed. That is why the absence of passwords in the field list should not be read as the absence of harm: the fields that are present (email, name, username and follower counts) are the ones that describe who someone is and how much reach they have.

Follower counts deserve a sentence of their own. They let anyone sort two hundred million rows by influence, which turns a general file into a list of journalists, officials, creators and brand accounts with the email address behind each one. Accounts like those are the targets of fake verification notices, fake copyright strikes and fake partnership offers, all of which work better when the sender knows the real address the account was registered with. If you run a large account, the practical step is to make sure that address is not one you publish anywhere, and to move the account to an address used for nothing else.
