Nothing in this breach was secret. Display names, avatars, follower counts and countries were all public on the platform, and the email addresses were not. The attacker's entire contribution was joining the two, for roughly a fifth of the user base.

That join is the product. A handle chosen precisely because it is not your name is worth very little on its own; attached to a mailbox you actually read, it becomes an identity and a delivery channel at the same time. Artists who released work under an alias and listeners whose taste they would rather not have attributed to them are in the same file, and no amount of care about what they posted would have kept them out of it, the private half came from the account system, not from anything they published.

The practical exposure is phishing with an unusually good hook. A message quoting your real handle, your follower count and your country reads exactly like the platform, and the platform is precisely who a working musician expects to hear from about payouts, copyright claims and takedowns. Open the app and look; do not follow the link, however specific the message is about a track you genuinely uploaded.
