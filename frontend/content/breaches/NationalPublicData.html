This is the entry that came with the "3 billion people" headline, and it deserves a careful reading because it is flagged as unverified. That flag is not a technicality. Have I Been Pwned's own description says the origin and accuracy of the data remain in question. Billions of rows are not billions of people: rows repeat, and one person can appear many times. The 134 million unique email addresses here are the more meaningful number.

The more important point is what National Public Data was. It was a background check service, a data broker, and most people in its files never had any relationship with it. They did not sign up, accept terms or choose to trust it. Their names, addresses, dates of birth and US social security numbers were collected and sold about them, then lost.

That is why the right response is not a password change. If you are in the US, a credit freeze at all three bureaus is free and stops new accounts being opened in your name, which is the main thing an exposed SSN enables. It also shows why removing yourself from people search and broker sites is worth the tedious effort: data that is not held cannot leak.

The record dates the breach to April 2024 and the HIBP addition to August 2024, and a background check service typically keeps historical addresses and records, so the details attached to a person may describe where they lived years ago, and the people most affected are those whose social security number is in the file, regardless of whether the address is current.

A credit freeze does not stop every kind of misuse. Tax refund fraud and medical identity fraud use an SSN without opening a credit account. In the US, the IRS offers an Identity Protection PIN that blocks someone else from filing a return in your name, and checking your benefits statements for activity you do not recognise covers the medical side. Together with a freeze, those are the controls that match what this file actually contains.
