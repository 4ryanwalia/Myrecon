The record mentions it almost in passing, and it is the most interesting thing in the entry: alongside the addresses and the bcrypt hashes, the breach exposed the titles of converted documents. Not the documents themselves. Their names.

File names are content, written by people who assumed no one else would ever read them. "Offer letter final signed", "divorce petition draft 3", "Series A term sheet", "biopsy results", "redundancy list Q4". Nobody names a private file euphemistically for their own benefit. A list of what seventy-seven million people were converting to PDF is therefore a map of what they were doing, legal trouble, job moves, medical news, deals in progress, indexed by email address and dated.

For companies that is targeting material of unusual quality: knowing an organisation converted a term sheet or a redundancy list in a particular week tells an attacker which pretext to use and exactly when to send it. For individuals it is an inference problem with no remedy. The password can be changed, and bcrypt probably held it anyway. The title of a document you converted five years ago cannot be renamed after the fact.
