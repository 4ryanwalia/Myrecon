Two details in this record deserve more attention than the headline number. The first is that it happened through a partner, which meant the 70 million people in the file had no relationship with the company whose name is on it, they bought glasses from a brand Luxottica owns, or had an eye test at an optician it supplies.

The second is the interval. The intrusion dates to March 2021. The data was sold on a hacking forum in late 2022. For roughly eighteen months the exposure existed and there was nothing for anyone in it to read, act on or be notified about. A company's statement that it is "considering other notification obligations" is the sound that gap makes.

Nothing in the exposed fields can be changed. Name, date of birth, address, phone number and gender are the permanent identifiers, and the practical consequence is a phone call, some years later, from somebody who already knows all five.

The partner route also explains why many people in this file will not recognise the name on the record. Luxottica sits behind a long list of eyewear brands and optical retailers, so the person whose details leaked may only ever have dealt with a high-street optician or an online glasses shop. If you received a notice and thought it was a mistake because you have never heard of Luxottica, it may not be. The relationship ran through a brand you did know.

That indirection has a practical edge. Scam messages after this kind of breach can name any of the brands in the group, or a generic "vision insurance" or "eye care" provider, and still sound plausible to someone who has had an eye test in the last few years. A date of birth and phone number on file are also what an insurance or benefits fraudster needs to sound legitimate. The defence is unglamorous: do not confirm personal details to inbound callers about prescriptions, insurance claims or refunds, and check any such contact against the provider whose receipt or card you actually hold.
