Two numbers describe ALIEN TXTBASE. Have I Been Pwned reports that the material obtained in February 2025 contained 23 billion rows. Across those rows, it identified 284,132,969 distinct email addresses. A row can repeat a previously seen address, including when the address appears against another website. Dividing the row count by the address count would tell you nothing reliable about how many accounts an individual lost. HIBP catalogue entry for ALIEN TXTBASE.

The name causes confusion in searches. HIBP files the collection under the ID AlienStealerLogs, while people write it as ALIEN TXTBASE, Alien Txtbase, AlienTxtBase or "alien text base". All of these refer to the same thing. HIBP says the data came from "a Telegram channel known as ALIEN TXTBASE", and Troy Hunt's write-up describes the corpus as 744 files published to that channel as free samples, with newer logs sold by subscription. Troy Hunt on processing the ALIEN TXTBASE logs.

Searches for a download or for the channel itself usually come from people trying to find out whether their own details are inside. You do not need the files for that, and fetching them means handling other people's stolen passwords and opening files from a criminal channel. Check your email address against HIBP or the MyRecon breach archive instead: the answer tells you whether you are listed and against which websites, without touching the raw logs.

Consider a fictional record that pairs reader@example.org with shop.example. The email domain in this example is example.org. The website domain is shop.example. Someone investigating the mailbox and someone responsible for the shop could therefore be looking at the same row for different reasons. Its presence says the two strings appeared together. Establishing when a particular device was infected, who operated it, or whether someone successfully signed in requires separate evidence such as device records and the shop's sign-in history.

There is a practical reason to keep those distinctions. After the initial publication, HIBP described additional work cleaning the domain associations and backfilling older collections. A count in a dated article and the domain associations returned by a later search can answer different questions. When recording an exposure for your own accounts, note the name of the collection, the date you checked it and the service you recognize. Keep passwords and full log entries out of support requests. HIBP's March 2025 follow-up on data quality.

If a listed service is yours, open it directly on a device you trust. Review active sessions and recent security events. Replace an exposed password and any copies you reused, beginning with the mailbox used for account recovery. If you suspect a work device, give your security team the collection name and the time of the alert so they can check the endpoint and account records together.
