Most people have far more accounts than they remember: forums from school, shopping sites used once, apps signed into with Google in ten seconds. Each one can hold an old password, a phone number, an address or a public profile under a username you no longer think about. This guide finds them in the order that turns up the most for the least effort, then closes them without breaking anything you still use.

The short version. Build a list from five places: saved passwords, your inbox, your Google, Apple and Facebook sign-ins, breach records for your email addresses, and a search of your old usernames. Then, for each account: export what you want, cancel any billing, move any sign-ins that depend on it, and delete it. Keep the confirmation.

Why old accounts are worth deleting

An abandoned account is still a target for credential stuffing whenever it shares a password with a service you use today. Old sign-up forms also retain sensitive details like dates of birth, delivery addresses, and recovery phone numbers.

Deleting old accounts reduces your exposed surface across both private breaches and public profiles. An abandoned forum or social profile under your current username lets strangers connect separate parts of your history, and closing it is the exposure you can directly control.

Step 1: Start with saved passwords

Your password manager or browser already holds a list of sites you have signed in to. Export or scroll that list first. It is the fastest source and usually the largest.

  • Browser-saved passwords in Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari list every site where you let the browser remember a login, including ones from years ago if you have synced the same profile since.
  • A password manager shows the same, often with the date each entry was created, which helps you spot the forgotten ones.
  • Write each entry into a private list with three columns: the service, the email or username used, and what you plan to do. Never copy the passwords themselves into that list.

Step 2: Search your inbox for sign-up emails

Almost every account sends a welcome, verification or receipt email. Searching for those finds accounts your browser never saved. In Gmail, these searches work as written (Outlook and most webmail accept the same words):

Inbox searches that surface forgotten accounts.
SearchWhat it finds
subject:(welcome OR "confirm your" OR "verify your")Sign-up and verification emails, the most reliable sign that an account exists
subject:("reset your password" OR "password reset")Accounts you used long enough to forget the password
subject:(receipt OR "your order" OR invoice)Shops that probably created an account during checkout
unsubscribe older_than:2yServices still emailing you that you stopped using years ago
"your account" older_than:5yThe oldest accounts, which are the ones most likely to share an old password

Repeat the searches in every email address you have used, including old school, work and ISP addresses you can still open. An address you no longer control is a problem of its own: see when you cannot sign in.

Step 3: Check Google, Apple and Facebook sign-ins

Accounts created with "Sign in with Google", "Sign in with Apple" or a Facebook login often never send a welcome email and never get a saved password. The sign-in provider keeps the list for you.

  • Google: open your Google Account's linked apps page and select Sign in with Google. Each app shows its details, and Stop using Sign in with Google removes the link. (Google's instructions.)
  • Apple: on an iPhone or iPad, open Settings, tap your name, then tap Sign in with Apple. Select an app and tap Delete to stop using it. (Apple's instructions.)
  • Facebook: the apps, websites and games you logged in to with Facebook are listed in your Facebook settings. (Meta's help page.)

Removing the link does not delete the account on the other site. It only stops that sign-in method. Note each app in your list first, then close the account on the app's own site. If you remove the link first, you may lose the only way you had to sign in.

Step 4: Use breach records as an account inventory

A data breach notice is unwelcome, but a list of the breaches your email address appears in is also a list of services where you had an account. Forums, games and shopping sites that closed or were sold years ago often show up here and nowhere else.

For every breach it lists, ask two things: does the account still exist, and did you use that password anywhere else? The second question matters more. Step 5: Search the usernames you used to use

Old handles are the accounts strangers find first, because a username search connects them in seconds. Write down every username you have used: gamer tags, forum names, the handle you had at school, variations with numbers or underscores.

Search each one with MyRecon's username search, which checks 560+ platforms on a free signed-in scan, or check a handle with the username checker. Not every hit is yours: a common handle will match other people. Open each result and confirm it is your account before you plan to close it. Searching the web for your old handle in quotation marks also finds forum posts and profiles on small sites that no checker covers.

Before you delete: four things to do first

  1. Export what you want to keep. Photos, messages, purchase history and files. Use the service's own download or export option, and open the file to check it worked before closing the account.
  2. Cancel billing separately. Deleting an account or an app may not stop a subscription that runs through the App Store, Google Play or a payment provider. Cancel there first and keep the confirmation.
  3. Move anything that depends on it. If other services sign in through this account, or send password resets to this email address, change them first. Closing an email account that other accounts use for recovery can lock you out of all of them.
  4. Remove personal details you can edit. On services that keep data after deletion, clearing your phone number, address and profile text first limits what remains.

How to delete old accounts on each service

Look in account, privacy, or security settings for a permanent delete option. Because no single delete online accounts tool can close accounts across third-party platforms for you, use JustDeleteMe or search the service name plus "delete account" to reach its official help page directly.

Read the confirmation screen. Many services keep the account recoverable for a period after you ask, and signing back in during that time can cancel the deletion. Our comparison of deactivating and deleting lists the recovery windows on X, Snapchat, Instagram, LinkedIn and Discord. Developer accounts need more care, because deleting one can free the username for someone else: see what happens when you delete a GitHub account.

Save the confirmation email or a screenshot with the date. Two to six weeks later, check whether the profile is still visible while signed out.

When you cannot sign in

Try the service's official account recovery first. If the recovery email or phone number is gone, contact the service's support or privacy team to state the account is yours and request closure.

Offer only details the owner would know, such as the username, approximate sign-up date, or a past order number, and follow their official verification flow. Never send identity documents to an unofficial address found on a forum; if the account is an impersonator, follow how to report impersonation instead.

A deletion request you can copy

Where a service has no delete button, a short written request usually works. Send it from the address on the account if you can. Residents of the EU and UK can cite the right to erasure in Article 17 of the GDPR; California residents have a right to delete under the CCPA. Elsewhere, ask anyway: many services honour the request regardless of where you live.

Subject: Request to delete my account

Hello,

I would like my account on [service] deleted, together with the
personal data associated with it.

Username: [username]
Email on the account: [email address]
Approximate sign-up date: [year]

Please confirm when the account and data have been deleted, and
tell me if any data will be kept and why.

Thank you,
[Your name]

What deleting an account does not remove

Closing the account removes the source, not every copy. Search results can take weeks to drop out, archives and screenshots may persist, and anything other people reposted stays with them. After the account is gone, ask search engines to drop stale results; our guide to removing personal information from search covers that step.

Deleting accounts also does not undo a past breach. If an old site leaked your password, the leak happened when it happened. What protects you now is making sure that password is not in use anywhere else.

Common questions

How do I find all my old online accounts?

Combine five sources: your password manager or browser's saved logins, an inbox search for welcome and verification emails, the apps listed under Sign in with Google, Sign in with Apple and Facebook login, a breach check of every email address you have used, and a username search for the handles you used years ago. No single source finds everything.

Is there a tool that deletes all my accounts at once?

No tool can delete accounts on your behalf across services, because each one needs you to sign in or prove ownership. Directories such as JustDeleteMe link straight to each service's deletion page, and tools such as MyRecon help you find the accounts. The deletion itself is one request per service.

What if I cannot log in to an old account?

Use the service's official account recovery first. If the email address is gone, contact the service's support or privacy team, say the account is yours and ask for it to be closed. Give only what their official process asks for and keep a copy of the request.

Should I delete or deactivate old accounts?

Delete accounts you will never use again. Deactivation usually only hides a profile and keeps your data. On some platforms deactivation is the first stage of deletion, so check the platform's recovery window before assuming the account is gone.
