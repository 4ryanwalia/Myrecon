What a breach checker can and cannot tell you

A breach checker compares your email address, phone number or password against data that leaked from companies in the past. A hit tells you three useful things: which service lost your data, roughly when, and what kinds of data went with it (passwords, phone numbers, addresses, security questions).

It cannot tell you whether anyone used that data against you, and a clean result is not a guarantee. Many breaches are never made public, some leaked datasets are never indexed, and different services load different datasets. Our article on what a clean breach check means covers this in detail.

We ranked these tools on four things: privacy of the check itself (do you have to give up your email or password to use it?), quality of the breach data, whether it alerts you to new breaches, and how clearly it tells you what to do next.

What to do after a breach hit

  1. Change the password on the breached service, and on every other site where you used the same one. Reuse is how one breach becomes five account takeovers.
  2. Turn on two-factor authentication, preferably an authenticator app or passkey rather than SMS.
  3. Watch for targeted phishing. Emails that mention the breached company by name are the most common follow-up.
  4. If financial or ID data leaked, follow the company's notice and consider a credit freeze where available.
  5. Note the date. The breach date and the disclosure date are often years apart; our explainer on breach versus disclosure dates helps you work out which passwords were exposed.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best free data breach checker?

Have I Been Pwned is the reference for breach data. For alerts on Android without handing over your email, we rank MyRecon first, and we publish it, because it compares the breach catalogue on your phone. Mozilla Monitor is a good free option for email alerts.

Is it safe to type my password into a breach checker?

Only if it uses k-anonymity. Pwned Passwords, and tools built on it such as MyRecon's password check, hash the password on your device and send only the first five characters of the hash, so the service never sees the password.

My email was in a breach. What should I do?

Change the password on the breached site and anywhere you reused it, turn on two-factor authentication, and watch for phishing that mentions the breached service. A breach result alone does not mean someone logged in.

Why do different breach checkers show different results?

Each service indexes different datasets and names them differently. One may list a breach another never loaded. Checking two sources gives a fuller picture, and a clean result means no known record, not proof of safety.
