“No breach found” means the completed check found no match in the sources it supports. It is not a certificate that your information has never been exposed. A timeout, unavailable provider or unsupported query is a different outcome and should remain unknown.

Read the result before taking action

Separate a verified negative lookup from an incomplete check or a direct company disclosure before deciding whether your account is safe.

Why a real notification may not appear in search

A catalogue can only expose records available to that service. Some incidents are not published there; some records use another address; some involve information that is not searchable by email. The Have I Been Pwned FAQ (checked 28 Nov 2026) explains coverage limits, sensitive breaches, and why certain compromises are never loaded into public search.

Several routine gaps explain a clean check alongside a real exposure:

  • Unpublished or private incidents: Targeted compromises, insider thefts, and ransomware extortion datasets that are never released publicly will not appear in breach aggregators.
  • Alias and legacy-address mismatch: Older signups often sit under a work email, university address, plus-addressed alias (name+shop@domain), or retired ISP mailbox rather than your current primary inbox.
  • Non-email identifiers: Breaches of medical records, payment processors, or telecom accounts may expose a phone number, customer ID, or postal address without indexing an email field.
  • Catalogue ingestion lag: A vendor may notify customers or file with a regulator weeks before a dataset is verified and added to searchable indices.

Use addresses you own and know you used. Do not guess other people's addresses or upload private correspondence to make a check more complete.

Keep three questions separate

Was my identifier in a published breach? Does someone currently have access to my account? Is a public profile mine? These require different evidence. A breach match does not establish a current login, a username match does not establish account ownership, and an unfamiliar session in your account settings is a reason to investigate even if your breach check is clean.

Concrete verification steps after a clean breach check

If you received a suspicious login alert, a phishing email referencing real personal details, or a vendor breach letter despite a clean search, run four direct checks you control:

  1. Check password exposure independently via k-anonymity: Test reused passwords locally through Pwned Passwords (checked 28 Nov 2026) or MyRecon's browser-side password checker, which sends only a 5-character SHA-1 prefix. Follow NIST SP 800-63B (checked 28 Nov 2026) by replacing any known-breached password immediately.
  2. Inspect active sessions and forwarding rules: Open your email provider's security log directly (never via links in an alert), sign out unknown devices, and check inbox filters or auto-forwarding rules that attackers use to hide password resets.
  3. Verify vendor notices through official channels: Navigate directly to the company's status or security page, or confirm regulatory breach listings such as the California Attorney General data breach search (checked 28 Nov 2026).
  4. Harden recovery and fraud defences: Enable app-based or hardware-key MFA per CISA MFA guidance (checked 28 Nov 2026). If financial or government ID data was disclosed in a vendor letter, use the step-by-step recovery checklist at IdentityTheft.gov (checked 28 Nov 2026).

A repeatable review record

Keep a small private note with the source, date, outcome and next action. Avoid saving leaked data, screenshots of unrelated people or full notification letters in public issue trackers. A useful entry reads “completed source check, no match, checked 28 Nov 2026; company notice reviewed separately.” It records what was verified without overstating safety.

Review unexpected security messages through the official service, update reused passwords through its own settings and inspect recovery options when appropriate. If a provider check failed, preserve that status until a later successful check resolves it. A green card should never hide a network failure.
