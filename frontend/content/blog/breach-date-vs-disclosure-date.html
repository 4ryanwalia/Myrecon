A breach alert received today can refer to an incident from years ago. Before deciding what happened, separate the date of access, the date the organisation disclosed it, the date a breach catalogue added the record and the date you received an alert. Each answers a different question.

Four dates, four meanings

Treat every breach timeline as four distinct timestamps so an old database dump added to a tracker today is never mistaken for a fresh intrusion.

DateWhat it tells youWhat it cannot prove
Breach dateWhen the recorded incident occurred, sometimes approximatelyThe day your individual record was accessed
Disclosure dateWhen the organisation or source announced the incidentThat access started on that day
Catalogue additionWhen a provider added the incident to its searchable collectionThat the organisation suffered another incident
Your notificationWhen you were informed or a monitor found a changeThat your account was used by somebody else

Why statutory notification clocks still leave long gaps

Regulators enforce strict reporting deadlines, yet those clocks normally start when an organisation becomes aware of a breach, not when an attacker first gained access. Forensic scoping, law-enforcement holds, and third-party vendor chains regularly add weeks or months between intrusion and customer letters.

  • EU GDPR Article 33 & UK GDPR: Controllers must notify the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of a reportable breach, as outlined by the European Commission data protection framework and the UK ICO personal data breach guide (checked 28 Nov 2026).
  • India CERT-In Directions (28 April 2022): Covered entities must report listed cyber incidents to CERT-In within 6 hours of noticing or being brought to notice of them under the CERT-In Section 70B Directions (checked 28 Nov 2026).
  • US state breach portals: Public registries such as the California Attorney General data breach list (checked 28 Nov 2026) publish sample notices showing both the discovered breach dates and the public filing date, letting you verify whether an alert is genuinely new.

Read an alert in order

Follow a five-step verification sequence whenever a new breach alert arrives so you confirm the source and date before changing account settings.

  1. Open the organisation's official notice independently of any link inside the alert message, or check a state regulator filing.
  2. Identify the incident period, when the organisation discovered it, and whether the breach date is marked as approximate.
  3. Record which information categories the notice says concern you (passwords, emails, phone numbers, government IDs, or payment fields).
  4. Compare the breach name and incident date with earlier notices or your password-manager history before treating it as a second incident.
  5. Write down the action you took, the date you rotated credentials, and when you will recheck it.

An illustrative example: a company reports a 2022 incident in 2026 and a catalogue adds the record a week later. A daily update should include that newly added record even though the underlying incident is old. Changing its breach date to 2026 would make the timeline less accurate.

How MyRecon labels the record

The public breach API keeps breach_date and added_date separate. The latest-record feed sorts by addition date, while an incident archive can sort by breach date. The feed's generated value describes the publication of that JSON response; it is not the date every incident happened.

These distinctions follow the Have I Been Pwned breach model (checked 28 Nov 2026). Provider coverage is limited to records it has received and published. A daily refresh cannot discover an incident that no source has disclosed.

Turn the timeline into a practical decision

Match your remediation to the actual age of the exposure and the fields listed. For an old password exposure, check your password manager to see whether that password was already replaced after the breach date or is still reused elsewhere; rotate any reused credential immediately and enable MFA per CISA MFA guidance (checked 28 Nov 2026).

For a personal-detail exposure (date of birth, address, or phone number), remember that static identifiers do not expire: audit which accounts use those details for recovery or phone verification. Keep uncertainty visible: an alert records known exposure, while unfamiliar sign-ins require a separate account review. Do not label every alert as a current account takeover.
