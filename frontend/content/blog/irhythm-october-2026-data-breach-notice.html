An October breach letter does not mean an October intrusion. iRhythm's October 2, 2026 update addresses unauthorized access that occurred in early June 2026, followed by four months of forensic review and individual record reconciliation before notices went out.

Incident and notification timeline

In its October 2 company update, iRhythm reported that data was accessed and downloaded between June 3 and June 8, 2026, from certain third-party-hosted business applications. The company detected the activity around June 8 and began mailing individual notices on October 2 to affected people for whom it held contact details.

That four-month gap between intrusion and notification is common in healthcare incidents, where organizations must parse unstructured files, identify distinct patients, and satisfy HIPAA breach-reporting duties tracked on the U.S. HHS Office for Civil Rights Breach Portal (checked 28 November 2026). See our explainer on breach date versus disclosure date for why timelines diverge.

What information was involved

According to iRhythm's disclosure, the affected business applications contained patient names, contact details, patient account numbers, device serial numbers, insurance numbers, dates of service, and dates of birth. The company stated that it does not retain individual financial-account or payment-card numbers in those systems, and had identified no impact to its monitoring products, clinical or medical-device systems, or patient safety.

Read your own notice carefully

Keep the original letter and check which data categories it lists for your specific record. A public press release describes the union of fields across all affected files; your individual notice tells you what applies to you and what credit or identity-monitoring support is offered.

  • Guard against medical-identity fraud: Review Explanation of Benefits (EOB) statements from your health insurer for unfamiliar dates of service, providers, or device claims. Exposed insurance numbers and dates of birth can be abused even when payment cards were not involved.
  • Verify outreach independently: If you receive a phone call, SMS, or email asking for a one-time code, Social Security number, or payment to claim breach support, hang up and call the number listed on your original notice or the company's official website.
  • Use official recovery checklists if misuse appears: The FTC's identity theft guidance and IdentityTheft.gov (checked 28 November 2026) provide step-by-step dispute templates for medical and insurance identity theft.
