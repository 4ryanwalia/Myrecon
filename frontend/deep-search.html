Deep Search

correlation console

Most username checkers ask one question: does this profile URL return HTTP 200? A great many sites answer 200 for every handle ever typed, which is how those tools produce confident lists of accounts that were never there. Deep Search checks 123 public platforms and tells you which of three things it actually established about each one, and says so plainly when the answer is that it could not tell.

Values in monospace are machine-verified facts; prose is interpretation. Every finding cites its source and carries a confidence band. MyRecon reports correlations between public accounts, it does not confirm identities.

How Deep Search decides something is a match

A username check is easy to run and easy to get wrong. Deep Search works from the opposite assumption to most tools: a response is evidence, not an answer, and every platform fails in its own particular way. A match has to rest on at least one signal that could only have come from a page rendered for that specific account: the handle in the page title, a follower count. Generic signals never qualify on their own, because a site-wide social image and a canonical URL that echoes back whatever path was requested score exactly the same for a handle nobody has ever registered.

That costs coverage, deliberately. A real profile on a platform that renders entirely in the browser scores nothing here, because its response is genuinely indistinguishable from a nonexistent one. Silence is the honest answer in that case, and it is the one we give.

The three tiers of evidence

  • Confirmed. The platform returns a profile containing the handle, and content unique to that profile, a display name, an avatar, a join date, a follower count. Something exists that could only come from a real account.
  • Probable. The response distinguishes a registered handle from an unregistered one, but returns nothing further. The account exists; nothing about who holds it is established.
  • Unconfirmed. The platform answers identically for a real handle and an invented one, a soft 404, a catch-all landing page, a login wall, or a rate-limit response dressed up as a result. These are reported as unconfirmed rather than quietly counted, because counting them is how other tools reach impressive totals that fall apart on inspection.

What the score actually measures

The correlation score answers one narrow question: how strongly do these accounts look like they belong to the same person? It rises with evidence that is expensive to fake or coincidental to share, a matching avatar image, a bio linking to the same domain, a rare handle, cross-links between two profiles. It falls for handles that are common English words, for platforms that permit anyone to reserve a name, and for matches resting on a single weak signal.

It is never a measure of identity. A high score says the accounts are related; establishing that a real person holds them is a separate exercise, and one this tool deliberately leaves to the investigator. Why there is no search by personal name

Because it does not work, and the failure is not obvious to the person reading the output. Guessing handles from a name, firstnamelastname, first initial plus surname, name plus birth year, returns whoever happened to register those strings, which on any large platform is almost never the person you had in mind. The results look authoritative, are wrong far more often than they are right, and the mistake lands on an uninvolved stranger. We would rather answer a narrower question accurately than a broader one badly.

The limits, stated plainly

  • Platforms change their responses without notice; a check that was reliable last month can start producing soft 404s at any time.
  • Results vary by network. The same query from a data-centre IP and from a home connection can return different answers, because rate limiting and bot detection treat them differently, explained in what a website's response actually tells you. This page runs from our server, so it is on the wrong side of that: the Android app runs the same checks from your handset and gets answered more often.
  • Absence of a match is not absence of an account. Private accounts, renamed handles and platforms we do not check all produce silence.
  • Nothing here reaches non-public data. If a profile is not visible to a logged-out visitor, it is not visible to this tool.

What it is for

Auditing your own footprint, which is what most people arrive to do, and authorised research where you have a legitimate reason to establish whether a set of public accounts are connected. It is not for profiling private individuals, and using it to harass, stalk or dox anyone is prohibited by our terms of use. If you are here to reduce your own exposure, the footprint guide is the more useful starting point.

The same method, from your own phone

The principle is the same one described above, a response is evidence, not an answer, and anything unverifiable is reported as unverifiable rather than counted. Two things are different, and both follow from where the app runs rather than from a longer feature list.

Every platform is checked against a control. The app picks one impossible handle at the start of a sweep and asks each platform twice, once for your handle, once for the one nobody could own. Only what the platform does differently between the two is counted, so a site that answers identically to both cannot produce a match at all. This page cannot work that way: doubling the requests from a single server address is precisely what gets a datacentre IP rate-limited.

It checks 559 platforms instead of 123, and gets a straight answer from more of them. Rate limiting and bot detection treat datacentre address ranges far more harshly than they treat phones, so a check this server is refused for is usually just answered for a handset. More coverage, and less of it ending in a shrug.

It also runs without us in the picture at all. The sweep goes from your device straight to the platforms being checked, no account, and the handle you are looking up never reaches a MyRecon server.
