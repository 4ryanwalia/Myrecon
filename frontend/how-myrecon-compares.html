How MyRecon compares

Four different kinds of thing solve this problem, and they are not really competing with each other, they are aimed at different people with different budgets and different tolerances for being wrong. This page describes the categories, what each one genuinely does better, and the two categories where you should pick something other than us.

No brand names below, on purpose. Pricing and behaviour in this market change monthly, and a page naming names goes stale or unfair within a quarter. The categories do not move. If you want to judge a specific product, the six tests on our legitimacy page work on any tool, including ours.

Subscription people-search apps

The largest category by revenue and the one most people meet first, because it advertises hardest. The shape is consistent: a free search returns something alarming-sounding, the detail sits behind a wall, and the wall opens for a cheap trial that renews at a price set out in text you did not read.

They are not all fraudulent, and the good ones have genuine depth in US public records, court filings, property, relatives, that nothing free can match. If that is what you need, they are the right category.

The structural problem is what the business model rewards. Revenue comes from alarm, and alarm comes from volume, so a long list of maybes converts better than a short list of certainties. That pressure sits on the accuracy of the results themselves. We wrote up the mechanics in the subscription-trap guide and what the data actually contains in what people-search apps find.

Paid OSINT and investigator suites

Professional tooling for people doing this as a job: fraud teams, due-diligence analysts, licensed investigators. They buy access to data nobody gives away, keep audit trails that hold up in a dispute, and are supported by humans.

If your work needs a defensible record of what you searched and when, this is the category and no free tool substitutes for it. The cost is real money, usually vetting, and a tool you have to learn.

Open-source CLI scanners

The serious free option, and the category where we are genuinely beaten on breadth. The well-known projects check thousands of sites; our web tool checks 100+ and our Android app checks several hundred. If your only question is "cover as many sites as possible and let me judge the output", install one of those instead. They are excellent and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

What you trade is interpretation. Raw output tends to be a list of URLs that returned something, and deciding which of those is a real account is left to you, which matters more than it sounds, because a great many sites answer "here is your page" for a handle nobody owns. You also need a terminal and a working Python install, which rules the category out for most people.

We tested this directly rather than assuming. Importing a large open-source catalogue into our own app added several hundred extra platforms and, on a real sweep, surfaced almost nothing the smaller hand-built list had missed, so the import was reverted and one useful entry kept. Breadth and usefulness are not the same axis.

Searching by hand

Always available, always free, and better than any tool at the thing tools are worst at: looking at a page and deciding whether it is really that person. If you have one specific question about one specific handle, opening the profile yourself is often the fastest correct answer.

It does not scale, and the failure is silent, you check the eight platforms you thought of and never learn about the ninth. Our search operators guide makes the manual route considerably more effective if that is the route you want.

Where MyRecon fits

One narrow claim: the results tell you how much they are worth. Every platform checked lands on one of three verdicts, confirmed, not found, or could-not-tell, and the third is reported rather than rounded into one of the other two. A platform that is rate-limiting us, serving a bot check, or showing the same page for every handle is listed as unverified with the reason attached, not counted as a find.

That sounds like a small thing and it is the whole product. A checker that counts HTTP 200 as an account will show you a longer list than ours and a meaningful fraction of it will be fiction. The measurement behind this is in why username checkers report fake accounts: of 24 platforms tested, six returned byte-for-byte identical responses for a real handle and an invented one.

The same logic runs through the breach lookups. If the breach services cannot be reached, the result says not checked rather than no breaches found, because those are different statements and only one of them means you are fine.

What “free” costs in each category

Nothing in this market is free in the sense of costing nothing. It is worth being explicit about what each category takes instead of money, because the payment is usually the part that is not advertised.

  • Subscription apps take your card details and, frequently, the friction of cancelling. Several also take the search itself, the address or name you looked up becomes a record they hold.
  • Paid suites take money openly, which is the cleanest arrangement on this page. You know exactly what the deal is.
  • CLI scanners take your time and your judgement. They are free of charge and free of interpretation, and the second part is the expensive one.
  • MyRecon takes advertising impressions and gives you a narrower result set. No card, no account, no stored search history on the server.

How to read any comparison page, including this one

A comparison written by one of the products being compared is, structurally, an advertisement. That includes this page. Three habits make them readable anyway:

  • Look for where the author loses. A comparison in which the author wins every row is not a comparison. If you cannot find a category where they concede, the page is not telling you anything.
  • Check whether the criteria were chosen to fit. It is easy to pick the four axes on which you happen to be strongest. Ask what axis is missing, here, the obvious omissions are total site coverage and access to licensed records, which is why both have their own row above.
  • Prefer testable claims to adjectives. “More accurate” is unfalsifiable. “Reports three verdicts and names which one it reached, and here is a handle you can test it with” is something you can prove wrong in a minute.

The axis that actually separates these tools

Strip away the branding and the pricing and one question is left: what does the tool do when it does not know?

Every category on this page has the same underlying problem. The public web does not reliably answer “does this account exist”. Sites return “here is your page” for handles nobody owns, block automated requests, serve bot checks, and hide profiles behind sign-in walls. No amount of money or coverage makes that uncertainty go away. The only thing that varies is what a tool does about it.

A subscription app resolves uncertainty toward a finding, because findings sell. A CLI scanner hands the raw response to you and takes no position. A paid suite typically buys its way around the problem with licensed data, which genuinely works and genuinely costs. MyRecon resolves it by saying so, which produces a shorter list and is the only one of the four that will ever tell you it failed.

There is no universally right answer among those. There is a right answer for a given job, and knowing which axis you are choosing on is most of the decision.

Pick something else if…

  • You need maximum site coverage. An open-source CLI scanner checks far more places than we do.
  • You need US public records. Court filings, property records and relative graphs are a licensed-data product. We have none of it.
  • You need reverse image or face search. We do not offer it at all, it requires a paid API and everything here runs on free, keyless sources. The guide points at the engines that do.
  • You need an audit trail. Nothing here is built to be evidence.

The honest summary

MyRecon is a free tool that tries hard not to tell you things it cannot support, at the cost of showing you a shorter list than tools that are less careful. If the shorter, better-labelled list is what you want, it is free and there is nothing to sign up for. If breadth or licensed data is what you want, two of the categories above beat us and this page has named which.
